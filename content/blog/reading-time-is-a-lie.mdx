Most reading-time widgets divide word count by 200 and round up. The number is presented with the
confidence of a measurement and the accuracy of a guess.

For a technical post with code, tables and diagrams, 200 words per minute is optimistic. For a short
essay, it is pessimistic. Nobody reads at a constant rate, and nobody reads your post at all until the
first paragraph earns it.

## Why keep it anyway

Because the alternative is worse: an unlabeled wall of text.

A reading estimate does three jobs that have nothing to do with literal reading speed:

- **It sets expectations.** "4 min" tells someone whether this fits in the gap before a meeting.
- **It signals effort.** Authors notice when a draft claims 14 minutes and cut it.
- **It helps scanning.** A list of posts with times is easier to compare than a list of identical
  paragraphs.

The value is in the relative ordering, not the absolute number.

## A slightly less dishonest formula

If you must compute it, account for the expensive parts:

````ts
const words = plainText.split(/\s+/).filter(Boolean).length;
const codeBlocks = (plainText.match(/```/g)?.length ?? 0) / 2;
const minutes = Math.max(1, Math.round(words / 220 + codeBlocks * 0.75));
````

Code is read slower than prose. So are tables. Images cost attention even when they cost no words.

Then label it honestly. "About 4 minutes" is a better promise than "4 min read", because it admits the
estimate is an estimate.

## The real constraint

The best reading-time widget is a strong opening. If the first two sentences tell someone why the post
exists, they will stay for as long as it takes.

Everything else is metadata.
