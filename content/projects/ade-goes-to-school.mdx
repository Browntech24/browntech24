## The brief

The story opens with a sentence a child can read on their own: "Ade goes to school." From there it follows
eight-year-old Ade out of his home in Ilode, across the red laterite road, through the morning market and
over the footbridge to the bell of St. Jude's Academy.

A wall of text with a quiz bolted to the end would have flattened all of that. The brief was to make the
story readable alone or with a parent, and to give a reader who stumbles somewhere to turn: audio, a
glossary, questions that check understanding, and a reason to keep reading past the last page.

## Approach

- **Read first, chrome second.** Warm paper tones, a serif text column and generous line height. Controls
  stay out of the way until they are needed: a Listen button, a settings panel, and tabs for chapters,
  cultural notes, comprehension and the story studio.
- **Narration that follows the page.** Playback reads one paragraph at a time and highlights the paragraph
  being spoken, so audio and text never drift apart.
- **Settings that stick.** Theme, typeface and text size are stored in `localStorage`, so a returning
  reader gets their own page back rather than the default one.
- **A glossary, not a footnote dump.** Ten terms — laterite, puff-puff, charcoal iron — each with a
  pronunciation, a plain definition and a short note on why it matters in the setting.
- **A way to keep going.** The story studio asks Gemini for a new scene in a chosen tone, or lets a reader
  put a question to one of the characters.

## How it is built

A React 19 single-page app built with Vite, styled with Tailwind CSS 4, and deployed on Vercel. Story
content lives in typed data (`StoryChapter`, `GlossaryItem`, `ReflectionQuestion`), which means the
reader, the quiz, the glossary and the export all read from one source instead of four copies.

The export panel turns the same data into Markdown, plain text or a printed page. That was the cheapest
useful feature in the project: a parent can hand a child a printable copy without anyone maintaining a
second document.

Two Express endpoints hold the AI features. `/api/story/expand` writes a new chapter from a prompt, tone
and focus character; `/api/story/character-qa` answers in a character's voice. They call Gemini 3.8 Flash
with the setting and cast as a system instruction. Locally they run from `server.ts`; on Vercel a
catch-all function reuses the same Express app, so there is no second implementation to keep in sync.

Narration uses the browser's speech synthesis, chained paragraph by paragraph:

```ts
utterance.onend = () => {
  if (index + 1 < paragraphs.length) {
    setCurrentParagraphIndex(index + 1);
    speakCurrentParagraph(index + 1, rate);
  } else {
    setIsGloballyPlaying(false);
  }
};
```

One callback turns the Web Speech API into a hands-free chapter player. Each paragraph is its own
utterance, so skipping, pausing and highlighting stay aligned without hosting or stitching audio files.

## What I learned

- **Browser speech is a good trade.** It costs nothing and starts instantly, but voice quality varies by
  device and browser. Rate control and visible paragraph sync matter more than picking the perfect voice.
- **Typed content prevents quiet drift.** Because quiz answers and glossary links derive from the same
  story object, editing a chapter cannot leave the questions describing a scene that no longer exists.
- **Keep the API key on the server.** Both AI endpoints stay behind the Express layer, so the client never
  sees a credential and the failure mode is a clear message rather than a silent empty panel.

The story is live at [ade-s-story.vercel.app](https://ade-s-story.vercel.app), with six chapters, roughly
1,650 words and ten glossary entries.
